Self-supervised learning · SUTD · Team of 4 · Apr 2026

Snekboros: vision world models for Snake

An agent that sees only rendered frames, learns how the game evolves in a compact latent space, and plans by imagining futures before it moves.

Team result: 0.89 completion on a 4×4 board, against 0.00 for a random policy and 1.00 for an oracle. My 14×14 track: raised a scratch-trained encoder's probe R² from 0.436 to 0.622, against a 0.927 pretrained ceiling.

My role: I led the self-supervised representation work, including the collapse monitoring and phased training schedule used in the final paper, and ran a separate 14×14 transformer study.

Final report available on request
PyTorch · JEPA · SIGReg · VICReg · ConvGRU · ViT · Optuna
Read the write-up

The problem

Snake has simple rules, but one bad early move can trap the snake many steps later. That makes it a compact test of long-horizon planning, posed in OpenAI's 2018 Requests for Research. We made it harder: the agent sees only rendered pixels, never the grid state.

We used a JEPA-style objective (joint-embedding predictive architecture). Instead of predicting future pixels, the model predicts future representations, which avoids modelling pixel detail that doesn't matter for decisions. The catch is a known failure mode: the encoder can map every frame to nearly the same vector, which makes prediction trivially easy and the representation useless. This is called representation collapse, and most of my work was about detecting and preventing it.

How the team system works

  1. InputRendered frame
  2. EncoderCNN → latent
  3. World modelAction-conditioned ConvGRU, rolled forward 15 steps
  4. EvaluatorSurvival × food score
  5. PlannerBest of 1,024 imagined move sequences

At each step the planner samples 1,024 candidate move sequences, rolls each one forward inside the world model, scores them with the evaluator, and plays the first move of the best. This is model-predictive control in a learned latent space. SIGReg, a regulariser that pushes representations towards an isotropic Gaussian, guards against collapse.

What I contributed to the final system

  • Collapse monitoring. I added effective-rank tracking on the centred representations as a per-epoch training signal. When the rank drops, the run stops instead of wasting epochs. The final paper uses it as the system's collapse monitor.
  • Phased training. Training all three components together from scratch was unstable and often collapsed within 10 epochs. I designed the schedule the paper uses: 20% of epochs on the encoder alone, 20% on the world model alone, then 60% jointly.
  • Experiment standard. Every ablation had to ship with saved results, diagnostics and a paired analysis notebook before it was reported. The paper's five ablation tables went through this pipeline.

My own track: how close can a scratch-trained encoder get? (14×14 board)

The team paper used a 4×4 board. In parallel I ran a study on a 14×14 board with transformer world models. At this size frames render at 224 px, which matches the input size of DINOv2, an encoder pretrained on ImageNet. That made a fair question possible: how close can an encoder trained from scratch on Snake get to a strong pretrained one?

I measured this with a linear probe: a ridge regression from frozen embeddings to the snake head's (x, y) position. Frozen DINOv2 sets the ceiling at R² 0.927. Before running anything, I fixed a pass band of ±0.05 around that ceiling so the target couldn't move afterwards.

  • The recipe closed about 38% of the gap but didn't reach the band. An asymmetric projector plus a VICReg variance–covariance penalty took the encoder from 0.436 to 0.622.
  • Two ingredients are each essential. Removing the EMA target encoder, or the action input to the predictor, dropped the predictor's probe to 0.052 and 0.037. The rest of the setup wasn't the bottleneck: with a frozen DINOv2 encoder in the same scaffold, the predictor's probe reached 0.802.
  • My own collapse alarm missed this. The effective-rank ratio stayed around 1.19 throughout, far above the 0.5 kill threshold. The embeddings still spanned many directions; they just encoded nothing useful. Rank tracking catches dimensional collapse but not informational collapse, so a probe has to be in the loop too.

Alternatives considered: tuning on validation loss

I first tuned the projector + VICReg recipe the usual way, with a 20-trial Optuna search minimising validation loss. Probing the trials told the opposite story: within the study, lower validation loss went with worse probe scores. The search's best trial scored R² 0.328, while a configuration found outside the search scored 0.622. The penalties left easy minima where representations looked well-behaved but encoded nothing. Since then I search on a downstream probe rather than training loss, and this finding fed into the team's move from VICReg to SIGReg.

Limitations

  • Every ablation row is a single seed, so there are no error bars.
  • At 4×4, the DINO-WM baseline kept its pretrained encoder frozen. Fine-tuning it would have been a fairer comparison.
  • My 14×14 track didn't reach the pre-registered band, and time ran out before it could go into the submitted paper.
LLMs & evaluation · SUTD · Team of 5 · Apr 2026

Repairing C vulnerabilities with small open LLMs

Given only a vulnerable C function, generate a patch with a 7B model, then verify it by compiling with AddressSanitizer and re-running the exploit.

15.4% of 52 real CVEs fixed (Pass@5, llama3:8b, Defects4C), with no sanitizer logs and no given bug location. Every number comes from the evaluation harness I built.

My role: I built the dense retrieval module and the full evaluation harness, and designed the ablation study.

Code ↗
Python · PyTorch · CodeT5+ · FAISS · Ollama · AddressSanitizer · FastAPI
Read the write-up

The problem

The state of the art in vulnerability repair (SAN2PATCH) reaches 79.5% Pass@1, but it uses GPT-4o, the true location of the bug, and sanitizer crash logs. We asked a harder, more realistic question: how far can open 7B models get with only the raw function and predicted bug locations? The constraint isolates what structured ML signals add.

How it works

Three signals feed a structured few-shot prompt. A fault-localisation model scores each line, a classifier predicts the vulnerability type, and dense retrieval finds similar past bugs along with their fixes. An open model (llama3:8b, deepseek-coder:6.7b or mistral:7b, served locally with Ollama) writes a patch. The patch only counts if it compiles under AddressSanitizer and the proof-of-concept exploit no longer triggers.

What I built

  • Dense retrieval. CodeT5+ (220M) embeds about 150,000 vulnerable C functions from the MegaVul dataset. Embeddings are mean-pooled, L2-normalised and stored in an exact FAISS inner-product index, so search is exact cosine similarity. A query returns the 3 nearest vulnerable-and-fixed pairs in under 5 ms. The model loads lazily, and a missing index returns no examples instead of crashing, so retrieval is a safe optional signal.
  • Evaluation harness. Extractors for VulnLoc (35 cases) and SAN2VULN (13 cases) pull each vulnerable function out of messy real repositories, handling merge commits, tag-based fixes and dead mirrors. A runner loops LLM → apply patch → ASAN compile → exploit → revert, and reports pass@k with per-case diagnostics.
  • Correctness. I audited the harness, logged 8 bugs by severity and fixed the critical ones, including one that silently skipped cases and one that broke standalone runs. I wrote 34+ unit tests and 4 replication guides with exact commits and settings.
  • Ablations. Flags switch each signal on or off, giving five configurations from no signals to all three, so each signal's contribution can be measured.

Alternatives considered

Exact vs approximate search. At 150,000 vectors, brute-force cosine search already runs in under 5 ms, so an approximate index would only have cost recall. Always-on vs adaptive retrieval. We shipped always-on retrieval, and the results show that was the wrong call.

  • The base model matters more than any signal. llama3:8b with no signals (13.5%) beat every deepseek and mistral configuration.
  • Retrieval hurt. Adding retrieved examples lowered the compile rate for every model. The likely cause is that few-shot examples crowd a small model's context and push the target function out of focus.

  • Sampling matters for small models. Near-greedy Pass@1 sat at 0–1.9%. Sampling five candidates lifted the best result to 15.4%.

What I'd change

  • Judge a component on the end task, not in isolation. Retrieval met every one of its own targets and still made the pipeline worse. Next I'd only inject examples above a similarity threshold.
  • I ran VulnLoc and SAN2VULN myself but only reached partial results (around 14–17% on VulnLoc) before networking problems on our two-machine setup blocked a clean run. Only Defects4C is reported.
Applied LLMs · Production · SUTD Capstone · Team of 7 · Oct 2025 – Apr 2026

CC+: AI summaries of customer feedback

A feedback platform for a B2B construction-supply company in Singapore. It collects WhatsApp and form feedback, classifies it, and writes a weekly digest for account managers.

Deployed and handed over to the industry partner in April 2026. Weekly summaries are cached and load in under 5 seconds.

My role: I owned the AI summariser from research to production, set up CI/CD, and wrote all 13 handover documents.

Built under an NDA, so no code, data or screenshots are shown.

Python · Claude API · Hugging Face · Node.js · React · PostgreSQL · GCP Cloud Run · Cloud Build
Read the write-up

The problem

Most of the company's revenue comes from repeat orders, but feedback arrived through WhatsApp and phone calls and lived in individual employees' heads. Each account manager looked after dozens of customers, so problems could go unnoticed until they cost a project. The goal was to collect feedback in one place, make it measurable, and surface what needs action.

How the system works

Feedback arrives from WhatsApp, online forms and on-site visits. A classification engine, built by my AI teammate, tags each message by aspect (product, service, delivery or payment), sentiment, and whether it needs action. Every week my summariser turns that feedback into a short digest for each aspect, shown on a web dashboard and an Android app.

The summariser

  1. Fetch the week's messages along with their classification results.
  2. Hand them to a Python worker, which computes signals for each aspect: message count, average sentiment and overall label.
  3. Send the signals and raw messages to Claude with a fixed, structured prompt (temperature 0) that requires five sections.
  4. Parse the response, store it in PostgreSQL, and serve the cached copy on page load.
  • Process isolation. The Node.js backend runs the Python worker as a separate process, so several hundred MB of model weights never sit in the web server and a model crash can't take the API down.
  • Caching and fallback. A scheduled job writes each week's summary once. Pages load the stored copy in under 5 seconds without calling the model. If the current week has no feedback, the dashboard shows the last non-empty week, clearly labelled as older.
  • Production fixes. Real usage exposed database connection-pool exhaustion under concurrent requests, and an empty-week check that counted messages instead of content. I fixed both and added an admin-only regenerate button.
  • CI/CD and handover. Cloud Build runs tests on every pull request and deploys on merge. I wrote all 13 handover documents, organised with the Diátaxis framework (tutorials, how-to guides, reference and explanation), including 22 tracked tech-debt items.

Alternatives considered

The summariser went through three stages, each driven by how the previous one failed:

  1. Extractive and abstractive models (BART, PEGASUS, FLAN-T5). Extraction produced disconnected fragments, BART and PEGASUS wrote in an overly formal register for construction-site feedback, and FLAN-T5 didn't hold the structure.
  2. GPT-4o-mini vs Claude. Claude scored higher on faithfulness and coverage, judged independently by GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro, and offered better quality for the cost at projected scale.
  3. Structured prompt plus classification signals. Passing the per-aspect numbers alongside the raw text gave the strongest results on every dimension, and is what shipped.

The jump from an unstructured prompt to a structured, signal-grounded one was bigger than the difference between the two models.

Evaluation

I built a provider-agnostic evaluation tool that compares Claude and GPT models on the platform's tasks, with a router that sends each request to the right provider.

Limitations

  • I designed an 8-condition benchmark with ablations that isolate the prompt and the signals, but only verified it on the first batch before handover. The production choice rests on the staged comparison above, not on a completed statistical benchmark.
  • Integration tests were scaffolded but not run, so coverage relies on unit tests and manual testing.
NLP & interpretability · SUTD Natural Language Processing · Group project · Nov – Dec 2025

How far does English GPT-2 transfer? Multilingual NLI

Our group implemented GPT-2 and fine-tuned it for natural language inference in English. I tested how well that transfers to 14 other languages, and what predicts the result.

Tokenizer fertility predicts zero-shot accuracy across 15 languages (Spearman −0.818). Fine-tuning lifted the four chosen languages by 18–25 points.

My role: I did the multilingual study and all of the extended analysis. Implementing GPT-2 and the English baseline was group work.

Code: repository coming soon
PyTorch · GPT-2 · Hugging Face tokenizers · XNLI · scikit-learn
Read the write-up

Setup

Natural language inference (NLI) asks whether a hypothesis follows from a premise, contradicts it, or is neutral, so chance is 33.3%. The group implemented GPT-2 small (attention, transformer blocks and the AdamW optimiser), framed NLI as predicting a single label token, and fine-tuned on English XNLI: 78.02% test accuracy at baseline and 82.28% after tuning. The course was taught by Prof. Wenxuan Zhang.

What I did

  • Zero-shot transfer. I ran the English-tuned model on all 15 XNLI languages. English scored 78.02%, and every other language landed between 35.87% and 45.55%, barely above chance.
  • Fertility as a cheap predictor. Fertility is the number of subword tokens per word. A high value means the tokenizer barely knows the language. It ranges from 1.11 for English to 9.48 for Thai, and its rank correlation with zero-shot accuracy is −0.818.
  • Choosing what to fine-tune. I picked Spanish, French, German and Swahili for low fertility and higher zero-shot scores. Swahili adds a language outside the Indo-European family.
  • One model per language vs one for all. I fine-tuned a separate model for each language, and a single unified model on all five languages together.

Looking inside the model

  • Embeddings don't separate by language. A t-SNE of 3,000 sentence embeddings across 15 languages overlapped heavily (silhouette score 0.090).
  • Attention changes with depth. Both layers attend heavily to the first token, but the deep layer adds more structured attention over content words.
  • The signal is there, but prompting can't reach it. On a synthetic dataset of Singaporean-parent tone (care, disappointment, comparison and guilt), prompting GPT-2 scored 25.4%, which is chance for four classes. A linear probe on the same model's frozen embeddings scored 94.4%.

Limitations

  • A pragmatic-inference probe looked fine on accuracy (0.76), but its macro-F1 was 0.47 because it almost always predicted "contradiction". Accuracy alone would have hidden this.
  • The tone probe didn't transfer across languages: 97.7% in English but 25.0%, chance level, in Chinese.
  • The per-language numbers are best-checkpoint accuracies from the original notebook. The cleaned re-run will report every model on the same test split.
